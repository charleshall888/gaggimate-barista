# Espresso Tasting & Feedback Guide

A practical guide for evaluating espresso shots and providing feedback to improve your next extraction.

---

## The Sour vs Bitter Problem

In sensory science, "sour-bitter confusion" is a documented phenomenon. It is difficult to distinguish between sourness and bitterness because they can produce similar unpleasant sensations. This leads to misdiagnosis when dialing in espresso shots. Here is guidance from Lance Hedrick on how to tell them apart:

| | **Sour** (Under-extracted) | **Bitter** (Over-extracted) |
|---|---|---|
| **Timing** | Hits immediately, fades fast ("high-toned") | Creeps in slowly, lingers ("low-toned") |
| **Texture** | Sharp, puckering, makes you salivate | Drying, astringent, clawing |
| **Think of** | Biting a lemon, unripe fruit | Over-steeped tea, grapefruit pith |
| **Shot was probably** | Too fast, too coarse, too cold | Too slow, too fine, too hot |

**Quick tests:**
- Does your mouth water? → Probably sour
- Does your mouth feel dry and the sensation linger? → Probably bitter
- Does it taste salty or savory? → Under-extracted (this is a key indicator many miss!)

**Common trap:** Many people say "bitter" when they mean "unpleasant." If the shot was fast and the taste hits hard and fades quickly, it's sour even if you instinctively call it bitter.

**Why timing matters more than tongue location:** The "tongue map" (sour on sides, bitter on back) is a myth from 1901. Taste receptors are spread across your entire tongue. Focus on *when* and *how long* the sensation lasts instead.

## How to Taste Your Shot

1. **Stir it first** — Espresso separates in the cup; mixing integrates the layers
2. **Slurp it** — Aerating spreads it across your palate
3. **Let it sit on your tongue** — Notice where flavors hit
4. **Breathe out through your nose** — This reveals more complex flavors
5. **Note the finish** — What lingers after you swallow?

**Taste at different temperatures:** Flavors change as espresso cools. A shot that tastes good hot but unpleasant cool may have hidden extraction issues.

---

## The Four Things to Evaluate

### 1. Balance
Is the shot sour, bitter, or balanced?
- **Sour** → needs more extraction
- **Bitter** → needs less extraction  
- **Balanced** → sweetness present, neither sour nor bitter dominates

### 2. Body
How does it feel in your mouth?
- **Thin/watery** → likely under-extracted
- **Syrupy/coating** → good extraction
- **Heavy but hollow** → possibly over-extracted

### 3. Sweetness
Can you taste sweetness?
- **None** → under-extracted (sugars not dissolved)
- **Present** → good sign of proper extraction

### 4. Finish
What happens after you swallow?
- **Short/abrupt** → under-extracted
- **Pleasant lingering** → well-extracted
- **Dry/astringent** → over-extracted

## Quick Feedback Template

When logging shot notes or asking for help dialing in, capture these essentials:

```
Rating: _/5
Balance: sour / balanced / bitter
Body: thin / good / heavy
Sweetness: none / some / good
Finish: short / clean / dry
One thing to improve: _______________
```

**Example:**
```
Rating: 3/5
Balance: slightly sour
Body: good
Sweetness: some
Finish: clean but short
One thing to improve: more sweetness, reduce sourness
```

## What Your Taste Tells You

| You taste... | Likely cause | Try... |
|---|---|---|
| Sour + thin | Under-extraction | Finer grind, higher temp |
| Sour + fast shot | Grind too coarse | Finer grind |
| Salty or savory | Severe under-extraction | Increase yield by 5g, or finer grind |
| Bitter + slow shot | Over-extraction | Coarser grind |
| Bitter + dry finish | Severe over-extraction | Decrease yield by 5g, lower temp, shorter ratio |
| Flat/lifeless | Stale beans or wrong temp | Check freshness, adjust temp |
| Good but lacks body | Ratio too long or channeling | Shorter ratio, better puck prep |
| Harsh/ashy | Over-extracted or dark roast issue | Coarser grind, lower temp, pressure decline |

## Common Flavor Descriptors

Use these if you want to be more specific:

**Positive:**
- Fruity (berry, citrus, stone fruit)
- Sweet (caramel, honey, chocolate)
- Nutty (almond, hazelnut)
- Floral (jasmine, rose)

**Negative (defects):**
- Sour, acidic, sharp (under-extracted)
- Bitter, burnt, ashy (over-extracted)
- Dry, astringent (over-extracted)
- Flat, lifeless (stale or wrong temp)
- Watery, thin (under-extracted)

## Notes for Different Shot Styles

The balance expectations differ by style:

| Style | Expected Character | "Off" would be... |
|---|---|---|
| **Traditional** (9 bar, 25-35s) | Full body, balanced, syrupy | Thin, watery, or harsh |
| **Turbo** (6 bar, 10-20s) | Sweeter, less body, clarity | Muddy or bitter |
| **Allongé** (longer ratio) | Lighter body, bright | Watery without complexity |
| **SOUP** (<2 bar) | Tea-like, juicy, clear | Thin without flavor |

Don't judge a turbo shot for lacking body — that's expected. Judge it on clarity and sweetness.

## The SCA Coffee Flavor Wheel

The [SCA Coffee Taster's Flavor Wheel](https://sca.coffee/research/coffee-tasters-flavor-wheel), updated in 2016 with World Coffee Research, is the industry standard reference for describing coffee flavors. It has three concentric tiers:

- **Inner ring (9 broad categories):** Roasted, Spices, Nutty/Cocoa, Sweet, Floral, Fruity, Sour/Fermented, Green/Vegetative, Other
- **Middle ring:** Sub-groups within each category (e.g., Fruity splits into Berry, Dried Fruit, Citrus Fruit, Other Fruit)
- **Outer ring:** Specific descriptors (e.g., Berry becomes Blackberry, Raspberry, Blueberry, Strawberry)

### How to Use It

Start at the center and work outward. You don't need to reach the outer ring — stopping at the middle ring ("fruity" or "berry") is perfectly valid. The farther out you go, the more specific the description.

**Gaps between categories are meaningful.** Connected cells (no gap) indicate closely related flavors. Gaps indicate more distinct flavor families. This design reflects professional tasting panel data, not arbitrary groupings.

### Common Pathways for Espresso

| Coffee Type | Typical Pathway |
|---|---|
| Ethiopian Natural | Fruity → Berry → Blueberry/Strawberry |
| Washed Ethiopian | Floral → Jasmine/Bergamot, or Fruity → Citrus → Lemon |
| Colombian Washed | Sweet → Caramel, Nutty/Cocoa → Chocolate |
| Brazilian Natural | Nutty/Cocoa → Chocolate → Dark Chocolate, Sweet → Brown Sugar |
| Kenyan Washed | Fruity → Citrus → Grapefruit, or Fruity → Berry → Blackcurrant |
| Anaerobic/Experimental | Fruity → Dried Fruit → Raisin, or Sour/Fermented → Winey |

Don't be intimidated by the wheel — it's a reference tool, not a test. Use it when you taste "something fruity" and want to pin down what kind of fruit. An interactive version is available at [notbadcoffee.com](https://notbadcoffee.com/flavor-wheel-en/).

## Palate Development Exercises

### Sour vs Bitter Calibration

Sour-bitter confusion is the #1 barrier to effective dialing-in. Train your palate with intentional extremes:

1. **Pull a deliberately under-extracted shot:** Use a visibly too-coarse grind. The shot should run fast (~15 seconds), taste sharply sour, and lack sweetness. This is pure under-extraction.
2. **Pull a deliberately over-extracted shot:** Use a very fine grind. The shot should choke or run very slowly (~45+ seconds), taste bitter, dry, and astringent. This is pure over-extraction.
3. **Taste them side by side.** Focus on the *timing* and *texture* differences (see the Sour vs Bitter table at the top of this guide).
4. **Repeat periodically** — even experienced tasters benefit from recalibrating.

### Temperature-Dependent Flavor

Espresso flavor changes dramatically as it cools. Tasting at different temperatures reveals different aspects of extraction quality:

| Drinking Temperature | What You Perceive |
|---|---|
| **~70°C (just poured)** | Aroma most intense. Bitterness and roast character dominate. Too hot for reliable flavor evaluation. |
| **~55°C** | Bitterness peaks. Body feels heaviest. Sweetness still masked. |
| **~50°C** | Transition zone. Bitterness begins decreasing. Acidity emerges. Defects start to reveal themselves. |
| **~44°C** | **Best evaluation temperature.** Sweetness peaks. Complex flavors emerge. Balanced shots taste their best here. |
| **~40°C** | Bitterness at minimum. Acidity and clarity most apparent. Under-extracted shots become unpleasantly sour. |
| **Below 35°C** | A well-extracted shot still tastes sweet and complex. A poorly extracted shot tastes increasingly unpleasant. |

**Key insight:** All coffee gets more acidic as it cools. Properly extracted coffee also gets *sweeter*. If your shot tastes good hot but sour when cool, it may be under-extracted. If it stays balanced or improves as it cools, your extraction is on point.

**Exercise:** Pull your best current shot and taste it at three points: immediately after pulling, after 1 minute, and after 2-3 minutes. Note what changes. A well-extracted shot should reveal more sweetness and complexity as it cools.

### Cupping Espresso (Bypass Dilution)

When you can't tell whether a shot is "bitter because it's over-extracted" or "bitter because it's concentrated," dilute it:

1. Pull your shot as normal
2. Add an equal volume of hot water (~90°C)
3. Taste the diluted shot

**Interpretation:**
- **Tastes clean and sweet when diluted** → The shot was well-extracted, just intense. Your ratio might be too short — try a longer ratio.
- **Still tastes bitter, dry, astringent when diluted** → Genuinely over-extracted. The problem is extraction, not strength.
- **Tastes thin and sour when diluted** → Under-extracted. The concentration was masking how little sweetness was dissolved.

This technique separates *extraction quality* from *strength*. Use it when you're stuck between "too strong" and "over-extracted" — they feel similar but require opposite fixes.

> *Always dial in on straight espresso, even if you usually drink milk drinks. Milk masks extraction flaws. See `MILK_AND_DRINKS.md`.*

## Aroma Evaluation

Around 80% of what we perceive as "flavor" is actually smell. Aroma evaluation isn't optional — it's the majority of the sensory experience.

### Two Pathways of Smell

- **Orthonasal (sniffing):** Breathing in through your nose. The external aromas rising from the cup reach your olfactory system. This is what you smell *before* tasting.
- **Retronasal (breathing out):** Volatile compounds travel from the back of your mouth up through your nasal passage when you swallow or exhale. This is what you experience *during and after* tasting. Retronasal perception can reveal flavors you completely miss by sniffing alone.

### The Nose Break Technique

1. **Before stirring:** Hold the cup near your nose and inhale gently. Crema acts as an aromatic lid, trapping volatile compounds underneath.
2. **Stir or swirl:** Break the crema to release a burst of trapped aromas. Inhale immediately — this "nose break" is the most concentrated aromatic snapshot of the shot.
3. **Smell again after stirring:** Aromas evolve as espresso cools and compounds change.

### Retronasal Technique

Take a small sip, let it coat your palate, then exhale gently through your nose with your mouth closed. The "slurp" technique (aerating the espresso as you sip) delivers aromatic compounds to your olfactory system almost instantly, which is why professional tasters do it.

### Empty Cup Assessment

After finishing, smell the empty cup. Residual aromatics can reveal sweetness (caramel, chocolate), floral notes, or defects (smoky, rubbery, stale) that were masked during drinking.

## Salami Shot as Sensory Diagnostic

The salami shot (described in `ESPRESSO_BREWING_BASICS.md`) is also a powerful sensory development tool. Tasting each extraction phase separately trains your palate to recognize what acids, sugars, and bitter compounds taste like in isolation.

**What each phase teaches your palate:**

| Phase | Key Compounds | Flavor Character |
|---|---|---|
| Early (0-10s) | Acids (citric, malic, chlorogenic) | Sharp, bright, sour, concentrated |
| Middle (10-20s) | Sugars, oils, Maillard compounds | Sweet, round, balanced, complex |
| Late (20-30s+) | Tannins, phenolic compounds, cellulose | Bitter, astringent, thin, drying |

**Training value:** Once you can identify these characters individually, you'll recognize them more easily when they're blended in a normal shot. A hint of sourness that you couldn't name before becomes recognizable as "early extraction character" — and you know to grind finer or extend pre-infusion.

## Off-Flavor Identification

Not every bad taste is an extraction problem. Some are defects in the beans, water, or equipment. The key question: **does the off-flavor persist when you change extraction variables?**

| Off-Flavor | Likely Cause | Diagnostic |
|---|---|---|
| **Woody/papery** | Stale beans (>4-5 weeks off roast) or quaker beans | Flat, cardboard-like, empty. Persists across all recipes. Check roast date. |
| **Rubbery** | Robusta content, or defective cherries | Burnt-rubber note. Persistent across all extraction settings. |
| **Musty/moldy** | Storage humidity, improper drying at origin | Swampy, damp character. Affects every cup from this bag. Check storage conditions. |
| **Metallic** | Water quality — iron, copper from old pipes, or limescale | Appears across ALL coffees, not just one bag. Test water (ideal: 75-250 ppm TDS, zero iron, pH 6.5-7.5). |
| **Salty/brothy** | Severe under-extraction | Early acids dominate without balancing sugars. Extraction likely below 18%. Fix: grind finer, increase yield. |
| **Ashy/burnt** | Over-roasted beans or scorched during roasting | Persists regardless of grind/ratio/temp changes. The roast, not you. |
| **Fermented/winey (unpleasant)** | Processing defect — over-fermentation at origin | Vinegary, acetic, briny. Present in every cup from this bag. Distinct from *pleasant* winey notes in well-processed naturals. |
| **Potato** | Antestia bug damage (East African coffees: Rwanda, Burundi, DRC) | Smells like raw potato when ground. Caused by bacteria carried by the antestia bug. Undetectable before roasting. One affected bean can taint an entire shot. |

### Defect vs Extraction Issue: The Test

1. **Change grind, ratio, and temperature** — make 2-3 significantly different shots
2. **If the off-flavor disappears or changes character** → extraction issue. You can fix it.
3. **If the off-flavor persists identically across all recipes** → bean defect, water problem, or equipment cleanliness. The fix is outside the recipe.

For water issues (metallic, flat, chalky across all coffees), the fix is water treatment — activated carbon filter, reverse osmosis + remineralization, or mineral-balanced water.

## Palate Fatigue & Management

### Why It Happens

Taste receptors become overwhelmed after continuous exposure to similar stimuli. It's primarily a brain phenomenon — neural adaptation reduces the release of signaling chemicals to prevent sensory overload. Caffeine itself also affects perception after multiple shots.

### Practical Limits

- **Shots 1-2:** Most reliable for nuanced evaluation. Your palate is freshest.
- **Shot 3:** Still decent, especially with palate cleansing between shots.
- **Shots 4+:** Increasingly unreliable for subtle differences. Focus only on the big picture (sour/bitter/balanced), not fine detail.
- **Trust your first impression** of each shot rather than prolonged analysis.

### Palate Cleansing

Between shots, use this sequence:

1. Sip sparkling water (carbonation scrubs the palate more aggressively than still water)
2. Eat a plain cracker or piece of bread (absorbs residual oils)
3. Wait 30-60 seconds
4. Sip room-temperature still water

Other effective cleansers: apple slices (mild acidity resets the palate), plain banana (calms caffeine jitters). Avoid strong flavors (citrus juice, mints) that leave their own residue.

### When Dialing In Takes Many Shots

If you're 4+ shots into a dialing session, take a 10-15 minute break. Walk away, drink water, and come back. Alternatively, keep your best shot parameters noted and revisit the next day with fresh taste buds — sometimes the best diagnostic tool is a rested palate.
